Title: Marine Infrastructure Planning

Published: October 2026

# Introduction
The seabed around many countries is becoming increasingly congested. Offshore wind farms, interconnectors, telecommunications (telecoms) cables, and pipelines are often competing for the same routes and landfall areas. Projects will generally follow the closest available path of least resistance, considering cost, permitting, and installation risk. A suitable route must also account for existing infrastructure, seabed conditions, shipping, fishing, environmental restrictions, and access for installation and repair. The landfall site must then be suitable for trenching, Horizontal Directional Drilling (HDD), or another installation method, with enough space for construction and maintenance.

Earlier cooperation can help identify these constraints before individual routes are fixed. The Dutch Subsea Cable Coalition supports coordination between government, cable owners, infrastructure operators and data centres when considering future landings. The International Cable Protection Committee (ICPC) similarly promotes better planning and cable protection, while stressing the need for repair access and geographical diversity. Sharing landfall areas may reduce duplicated work, like permitting and surveys, but concentrating critical infrastructure can also create a single point of failure.

# Offshore and Onshore Infrastructure Requirements
Landing the cable is only one part of the problem. A workable shore crossing still requires a terrestrial route and an available connection to the wider network. The nearest transmission station may not have sufficient capacity, an available bay, or the ability to transfer the additional power through the wider system. Offshore infrastructure can therefore be technically complete while the terrestrial network remains unable to make full use of it. For example, transmission constraints currently limit how much Scottish wind generation can be transferred to demand centres farther south. When the network cannot accommodate the available power, the National Energy System Operator (NESO) may pay generators to reduce output. The Eastern Green Link (EGL) projects will provide additional subsea transmission capacity between Scotland and England, helping to reduce these constraints.

Cable technology also changes what is required at the landfall. An Alternating Current (AC) connection may need several export circuits and reactive compensation equipment, whereas a Direct Current (DC) connection may reduce long-distance transmission limitations but requires substantial converter infrastructure onshore. These options need different amounts of land, different terrestrial cable routes and different connections to the existing network. The transmission design should therefore be considered before the landfall is fixed, rather than after a route has already been selected.

# Infrastructure Demands, Capacity, and Supply Chain Backlogs
Different infrastructure projects have different requirements from the same limited coastal areas. Power cables need access to suitable substations, converter stations, and transmission capacity. Telecoms cables need secure landing stations and diverse fibre routes inland. Data centres may want to be near both electricity connections and international fibre, while pipelines require access to port terminals, industrial users, or storage sites. There is some overlap but each has its own unique requirements.

Equipment availability adds another constraint. Large power and converter transformers, switchgear, shunt reactors and High Voltage Direct Current (HVDC) equipment can have production lead times of several years. Their size and transport requirements can also affect site access and layout. A project may therefore have a suitable offshore route and consented landfall but still lack the equipment, connection capacity or terrestrial infrastructure required to operate. Marine planning needs to consider the complete system, including what connects at the landfall and whether it can realistically be delivered. The optimal route considers the offshore cable, landfall, receiving infrastructure, and wider network.

# Developing Collaborative Approaches
The Dutch Subsea Cable Coalition (DSCC) brings together government, infrastructure operators, data centres, and other users to support new cable landings in the Netherlands. Its work includes developing landing strategies, navigating permitting, coordinating stakeholders, and investigating neutral cable landing stations. Similar German energy hub proposals aim to coordinate offshore generation, interconnectors, and onshore connections, rather than planning each asset separately. The ICPC supports this general approach through closer consultation between government and industry and clearer deployment and repair processes. However, it also warns against clustering and route foreclosure, and recommends multiple, geographically diverse landings. In the UK, the Crown Estate is taking a similar systems-led approach through its Marine Delivery Routemap, which will use spatial data and scenario mapping to consider future demands on the seabed and coastline across sectors, instead of viewing individual developments in isolation.
