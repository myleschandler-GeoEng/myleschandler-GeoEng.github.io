# Soil Descriptions
Seabed geology is complex. Soil descriptions can be presented in a variety of ways and all vary significantly in the standards and styles of classification; for example, interpretation of Cone Penetration Tests (CPT) geophysical horizons, regional classification models, Side-scan Sonar (SSS) surface interpretations, Sub-bottom Profiler (SBP) horizons, and physical log descriptions. Yet, interbedding, layering, laminations, poor sorting and high heterogeneity, fragments (rock or shell), pore space, and rock quality can all have a significant impact on the engineering parameters and models used for assessments as the behaviour of the soil is influenced by these factors.

# Classification and Engineering Impact
In many real-world cases, soils can be a mixture of gravels, sands, clays, fines, and rock and shell fragments. These can not only skew measurements, such as the 50% Passing Diameter (D50) used regularly to classify and model soils for sediment mobility, but also make classification difficult. 

A common geotechnical engineering problem is having all these high-quality and variable datasets and then having to determine the best theoretical calculation model to apply to a sandy silty gravelly CLAY or a gravelly clayey sandy SILT, where there are only pure CLAY or pure SAND models to select. Model selection uncertainty can create a wide variety of results. To better understand the soil, further samples or laboratory experiments may be required, and even then, the choice between a pure CLAY model or pure SAND model for this soil becomes challenging. 

# Cable Engineering Example
For cable routes this is even more difficult as the current Cable Burial Risk Assessment (CBRA) guidelines determine suitable depths using very simplified soil models: weak clay or strong clay/sand. Advancements in this area of research have began but the models still utilise homogeneous materials to control test constraints, and anchor deployment field trials are sparse and in heterogeneous soils. Some companies (like Intertek) have advanced this to capture additional soil types, but these models suffer from the assumption of homogeneous soil parameters within an interpreted soil layer. Classifying a soil as medium strength CLAY using interpreted CPT parameters when the co-located vibrocore shows sandy gravelly silty CLAY results in uncertainty that the analysis must consider. Recent studies into anchor penetration have shed some light on this topic, and the Carbon Trust have recently closed tenders to update this guideline. 

Further hinderance is observed where the CBRA's simplified ground model is provided for further studies. For example, a Burial Assessment Study (BAS) where tool selection for installation the CBRA burial depths is assessed. The key soil information required for a BAS are different to the CBRA. Using the same simplified soil model would likely result in a poor BAS, because other parameters not required for the CBRA like gravel content or friction angle are not important. It is important that simplifying data considers the end-product of the study, and this should be reviewed where interpreted ground models are supplied.
